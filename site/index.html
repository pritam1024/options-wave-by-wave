      Module 1 · Foundations

      What an option actually is

      A contract that gives one side a right and the other side an obligation, for a price paid today.

      Calls and puts

      A call gives its buyer the right, not the obligation, to buy the underlying at a fixed price (the strike) on the expiry date. A put gives the right to sell at the strike. The buyer pays a premium for that right. The seller (also called the writer) receives the premium and takes on the obligation.

      Analogy

      A call is the token amount you pay to lock a flat at ₹80 lakh for 30 days. If prices jump to ₹90 lakh you use the right and gain ₹10 lakh minus the token. If prices fall you walk away and lose only the token.

      A put is car insurance. You pay a small premium every year. If the car is wrecked (the market crashes), the insurer pays you the difference.

      The parts of a contract

      • Underlying: the index the option is on. Here that is NIFTY 50 or Bank Nifty.
      • Strike: the fixed price in the contract. NIFTY strikes come every 50 points, Bank Nifty every 100.
      • Expiry: the last day. NIFTY has weekly and monthly expiries on Tuesdays; Bank Nifty has only monthly expiries (last Tuesday).
      • Premium: the option's price, quoted in index points. You pay or receive premium × lot size in rupees.
      • Lot size: how many units one contract covers. NIFTY is 65 and Bank Nifty is 30 from the January 2026 series. NSE revises these about twice a year.
      Real NIFTY example

      NIFTY closed at 25,800 on 20 Nov 2026. Suppose you buy one lot of the 26,000 call expiring next Tuesday at ₹120 (an illustrative price). You pay 120 × 65 = ₹7,800.

      If NIFTY settles at 26,400, the call is worth 400 points, which is ₹26,000, so you make ₹18,200. If NIFTY settles at or below 26,000, the call expires worthless and you lose ₹7,800. Your breakeven is 26,000 + 120 = 26,120.

      Moneyness: in, at and out of the money

      With NIFTY at 25,800, a 25,600 call is in the money (ITM): it already has 200 points of intrinsic value. The 25,800 call is at the money (ATM). A 26,200 call is out of the money (OTM): all of its premium is time value, the price of the chance that NIFTY gets there in time. For puts it is the reverse: the 26,000 put is in the money.

      How Indian index options work

      • European style: they can only be exercised at expiry. Before that you trade the contract in the market.
      • Cash settled: nobody delivers the index. At expiry, in-the-money options pay the difference between the settlement price and the strike.
      • Settlement price: from 7 Dec 2026, expiring index contracts stop trading at 15:30 and settle on the average of the index constituents from 15:00 to 15:30 (per NSE circulars reported in November 2026).
      • Money flow: buyers pay the full premium upfront. Sellers post margin with their broker, roughly ₹1.8–2 lakh per naked NIFTY lot.

      Module 2 · Pricing

      What sets the premium

      Premium = intrinsic value + time value. Intrinsic value is simple arithmetic. Time value is where the five inputs and the market's expectations live.

      Analogy

      An option premium is an insurance quote. It depends on how close the risk is (strike versus spot), how long the cover lasts (time to expiry), how stormy the weather looks (volatility), and a little on interest rates.

      The five inputs

      If this goes up…Call premiumPut premiumWhy
      NIFTY (spot)UpDownCalls gain intrinsic value, puts lose it
      StrikeDownUpA higher strike is harder for a call to reach
      Days to expiryUpUpMore time, more chances to move
      Implied volatilityUpUpBigger expected swings make both rights worth more
      Interest rateSlightly upSlightly downFutures trade above spot by the cost of carry

      Time decay is not a straight line

      Time value shrinks roughly with the square root of the time left. An at-the-money option with 30 days left loses only a little each day. In the final week the melt speeds up sharply, and on expiry day whatever time value is left disappears.

      From the research

      Since NIFTY's weekly expiry moved to Tuesday, the stretch from Friday's close to Monday's close removes about 28.5% of a weekly at-the-money straddle's value. Weekend days still count as time.

      Put-call parity and futures

      For the same strike and expiry, call minus put equals the discounted gap between the futures price and the strike: C − P = (F − K) × discount. That is why a long call plus a short put behaves like a long future (a synthetic long), and why traders back out the market's forward price from option prices. NIFTY futures normally trade above spot by the cost of carry, about the interest rate minus the dividend yield.

      The model in one paragraph

      Black-Scholes (and Black-76, its version on futures) prices an option from the five inputs, assuming prices move in a random walk with constant volatility. Every input is observable except volatility. So traders flip the model around: given the market price, they solve for the volatility that makes the model match. That number is the implied volatility (IV), and it is how options are really quoted and compared.

      Module 3 · The Greeks

      How a premium reacts

      The Greeks measure how the premium changes when one input moves and the rest stay still. They are your dashboard.

      Delta: the speedometer

      Delta is how many points the option moves for a one-point move in NIFTY. An at-the-money call has a delta near 0.50: NIFTY up 100 points, call up about 50. Puts have negative delta. Delta also works as a rough probability that the option finishes in the money, and as a hedge ratio: a 0.16-delta option behaves like 0.16 of the index.

      In rupees, one NIFTY lot with delta 0.30 gains about 0.30 × 65 = ₹19.5 per NIFTY point. Traders call a 16-delta strike "one standard deviation away", because it sits about one expected move from spot.

      Gamma: the acceleration

      Gamma is how fast delta changes. It peaks for at-the-money options close to expiry. A buyer is long gamma: profits accelerate in a big move either way. A seller is short gamma: losses accelerate when NIFTY runs.

      Theta: the melting ice cube

      Theta is the premium lost per day if nothing else changes. Buyers pay this rent to sellers every day. It is quoted per day in points, or in rupees per lot.

      Vega: the storm forecast

      Vega is the change in premium for a one-point change in implied volatility. When the market expects a storm, all options get dearer. After a scheduled event passes, implied volatility often collapses (IV crush) and option buyers can lose even when they guessed the direction right.

      From the research

      India VIX fell on all 14 measured Union Budget days: the uncertainty was priced in beforehand and released on the day.

      The trade-off nobody escapes

      For options of the same expiry, you cannot have positive theta and positive gamma at once. Earning time decay means being short gamma, so you lose on large moves. Owning gamma means paying theta every quiet day. Every strategy in this course is a choice of where to sit on that line.

      Delta hedging: neutralising direction

      Delta hedging means offsetting a position's delta with futures, so a small NIFTY move leaves you roughly flat. One NIFTY futures lot has a delta of 1.0 per option lot. If your short strangle on 10 lots has delta −0.4 per lot, the book is −4, so you buy 4 futures lots.

      Real NIFTY example

      On 26 Oct 2026, with NIFTY at 22,753, our model sold the 23,850 call and 21,750 put of the 23 Nov expiry. NIFTY then rallied 13% to 25,800 by 20 Nov.

      The short call alone was down about 1,735 points per lot, over ₹1.1 lakh. With daily futures hedging, the whole position was down only 124 points per lot, 7.3% of its margin. The hedge had grown to one long futures lot per strangle lot as the call's delta reached 1.

      Module 4 · Volatility

      Volatility is the real product

      Option traders buy and sell expected movement. Learn to compare what options price with what NIFTY actually does.

      Realised versus implied

      Realised volatility (also called historical volatility) measures how much NIFTY actually moved, usually the standard deviation of daily returns over 20 days, annualised. Implied volatility is what option prices say NIFTY will do. India VIX is NSE's 30-day implied volatility for NIFTY, built from the option order book.

      Turning volatility into points

      • One day: IV ÷ √252. A VIX of 16 means about a 1% typical daily move (about 260 NIFTY points at 25,800).
      • To expiry: spot × IV × √(days ÷ 365). That is the one-standard-deviation move; NIFTY stays inside it about 68% of the time if the model holds.
      • An at-the-money straddle costs about 0.8 × that move. That is the market's price for the average absolute move.

      What the option market is pricing

      The volatility risk premium

      Insurers charge more than the claims they expect to pay, because the rare bad year can be enormous. Option sellers do the same. The gap between implied and later realised volatility is the volatility risk premium. It is the only reliable edge we found in Indian index options.

      Skew: why puts cost more

      NIFTY options do not share one implied volatility. Out-of-the-money puts trade at higher IV than at-the-money options, because investors pay up for crash insurance. One-month puts 5% below spot have run about 2.9 IV points above at-the-money, steady since 2008, while calls 5% above run about 1.7 points below.

      From the research

      Buyers of NIFTY puts 10% out of the money lost 52% of their premium on average. Buyers of calls 5% out of the money made 44% on average. Near-money calls have been cheap and far puts rich.

      Term structure

      Normally longer-dated options carry slightly higher IV than near-dated ones (contango). In a panic, near-term IV jumps above longer-term IV (backwardation). Backwardation is a stress signal: that is when the near-term premium is richest and most dangerous.

      Events

      Options price scheduled events (elections, Budget, RBI policy, US Fed decisions) ahead of time. On 4 Jun 2024, the election-result day straddle priced about a 2.9% move and NIFTY fell about 6%. The 2014 and 2019 results were over-priced. One bad mispricing can wipe out many months of selling.

      Module 5 · Who wins

      Buyers, sellers and the maths of winning

      A high win rate and a profitable strategy are different things. Expectancy decides, and costs and tails decide expectancy.

      What SEBI found

      In FY26, 87.7% of individual F&O traders lost money (SEBI's profitability study, August 2026). In SEBI's sample, traders who only bought options made up 93% of the group and 89.9% of them lost. "Majorly option sellers" lost only 43.8% of the time, but their average result was a loss of ₹51.7 lakh, because the losers lost enormous sums. Prop desks and algorithmic firms took nearly all the profits.

      Analogy

      The option buyer is the lottery player: usually loses a little, occasionally wins big, and loses over time because the ticket is overpriced. The naive seller is an insurer in an earthquake zone with no reinsurance: collects steady premiums for years, then one quake takes everything.

      Expectancy

      Expectancy per trade = win rate × average win − loss rate × average loss. A strategy that wins 97% of the time can still be fragile, and one that wins 43% can be close to break-even. Try our real backtest numbers below.

      Costs in India, late 2026

      • STT: 0.15% of option premium sold, and 0.15% of intrinsic value on long options exercised at expiry. 0.05% of futures value sold (raised on 1 Apr 2026).
      • NSE charges: 0.03553% of option premium; 0.00183% of futures value. SEBI fee ₹10 per crore. Stamp duty 0.003% on buys. GST 18% on brokerage and charges.

      Why naive sellers blow up

      Every famous option-selling blow-up was leverage meeting a gap: Niederhoffer in 1997, LJM losing about 80% in two days and XIV losing 96% in February 2018, and Allianz Structured Alpha funds losing over 90% in March 2020. In our own data, an unhedged NIFTY 16-delta strangle won 78% of its trades, but its worst trade (COVID, 2020) lost 312% of the margin it used.

      Module 7 · Which strategy when

      Match the structure to the market

      Two questions decide most choices. Where do you think NIFTY goes? And are options cheap or expensive right now?

      Direction is the first axis: up, sideways or down. Volatility is the second: is India VIX low, normal or high against its own history, and is implied volatility above or below what NIFTY has actually been doing? When options are cheap, owning them (buying convexity) makes sense. When they are expensive, selling them does, with the risk defined or hedged.

      What our data says about the two axes

      The volatility axis is where the evidence is: selling when implied volatility exceeded realised paid, and the premium was largest when VIX was high. The direction axis is much weaker: none of the timing rules we tested (dips, breakouts, oscillators, trend filters) gave option buyers a reliable edge on unseen years. Use your directional view to choose the side and the strikes, and keep size modest when the trade depends on it.

      Special situations

      • Before a binary event (election, Budget, RBI, US Fed): IV is high. Compare the implied move with your own estimate. Buy a straddle only if you think the move is underpriced; otherwise wait and sell after the event, when IV drops.
      • Expiry week: gamma is extreme. Short options move fast against you, and from 7 Dec 2026 settlement uses the 15:00–15:30 average. Expiry days themselves have not been more volatile close to close.
      • Strong trend: static short strangles lose to trends. Hedge delta, or trade the trend with spreads.
      • Crisis: when VIX spikes above 30, the premium is largest but swings are violent. Our data's best selling came after spikes, sized small and defined.
      Module 8 · Elliott Wave × options

      Waves as a map of risk

      Elliott Wave describes markets as repeating crowd cycles. Used with options, its biggest gift is the invalidation level: the price at which your count is wrong, which is exactly where a defined-risk spread should put its protection.

      The pattern in five minutes

      Markets advance in five waves (an impulse: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5) and correct in three (A, B, C). Waves 1, 3 and 5 go with the larger trend; 2 and 4 are pauses against it. Each wave is itself made of smaller waves, which is why you read the count top-down: monthly, then weekly, daily and hourly.

      Three rules that cannot be broken

      • Wave 2 never retraces more than 100% of wave 1.
      • Wave 3 is never the shortest of waves 1, 3 and 5.
      • Wave 4 never enters wave 1's price territory (except in diagonal triangles).

      Guidelines that usually hold

      • Wave 2 often retraces 50–61.8% of wave 1; wave 4 often 23.6–38.2% of wave 3.
      • Wave 3 is often the extended wave, around 1.618 × wave 1.
      • Wave 5 often equals wave 1, or 0.618 × the distance from the start of 1 to the end of 3.
      • Alternation: if wave 2 is sharp, wave 4 tends to be sideways, and vice versa.
      • Corrections come as zigzags (5-3-5, wave C often equal to A), flats (3-3-5, B near the start of A) or triangles (3-3-3-3-3, usually in wave 4 or B).

      Using the count with options

      • Put protection at invalidation. If your bullish count fails below a level, make that level the long strike of a put spread or the stop for a call. The structure then loses most exactly when the count is wrong.
      • Let volatility choose the structure, and the wave choose the side. High IV after a wave-2 or wave-C decline favours selling put spreads. Low IV at a wave-5 high favours buying protection.
      • Always hold an alternate count. If the alternate is very different, use smaller size or a wider defined-risk structure.
      • Confirm with momentum and breadth. Wave 3 should show expanding breadth; wave 5 often shows momentum divergence.
      What the evidence says

      There is no peer-reviewed evidence that Elliott Wave forecasts markets. Batchelor and Ramyar (Dow Jones, 1914–2002) found Fibonacci ratios between waves no more often than chance. Counts are also subjective; two analysts often label the same chart differently.

      Our own tests of momentum signals (RMI crossovers) and dip-buying found no standalone edge on unseen years. Treat Elliott Wave as a way to plan scenarios and place risk, not as a prediction engine.

      Module 9 · Risk, sizing and Indian rules

      Stay in the game

      The edge in options is small. Surviving the bad week is what lets it compound.

      Size for the gap, not the margin

      Overnight moves carried about 43% of NIFTY's variance in 2020–26, and 58% over the last 250 sessions. Gaps above 2% happen on 1–3% of days; the largest recent ones were −9.6% (23 Mar 2020), −5.1% (7 Apr 2025) and +4.75% (3 Feb 2026). A position hedged at the close still takes the whole gap. So ask one question before every short-option trade: what do I lose if NIFTY opens 10% away tomorrow?

      Rules of thumb that held up

      • Risk per trade: cap the realistic worst case (the maximum loss, or the gap loss for naked positions) at a few percent of capital.
      • Margin use: keep at least half your capital free. Exchanges raise margins in a crisis, exactly when prices are moving against sellers.
      • Kelly: the Kelly formula overstates size for payoffs with rare large losses. Professionals use half Kelly or less.
      • Stops and targets: in our tests, profit targets and stops changed the win rate more than the result, and a 2× stop lowered the hedged strangle's Sharpe from 1.00 to 0.89.

      Indian margin and expiry rules

      • Margin is SPAN plus exposure margin. A Sep 2026 SPAN estimate (NIFTY 23,350): short straddle ₹1.97 lakh, short strangle ₹1.77 lakh, iron condor ₹73–78k per lot. Scale up about 10% at NIFTY 25,800.
      • Short options pay an extra 2% extreme-loss margin on expiry day, and calendar spreads lose their margin benefit on expiry day.
      • Premium is collected upfront. Per-order freeze limits are 1,800 units for NIFTY and 900 for Bank Nifty.
      • Long options exercised at expiry pay STT of 0.15% of intrinsic value.

      Module 10 · Our tested playbook

      The delta-hedged NIFTY strangle

      The one strategy that held up in both halves of 19 years of NSE data. Here are its exact rules, its results and its weak spots.

      The rules

      1. Entry window. When you hold no position, act on the first session on which the next NIFTY monthly expiry is 28–40 calendar days away.
      2. Volatility filter. Enter only if at-the-money implied volatility (India VIX is a close proxy) is above NIFTY's 20-day realised volatility. If not, check again each session while the window is open.
      3. Strikes. At about 15:20, sell the call and the put nearest 0.16 delta on that expiry.
      4. Hedge. Every session at about 15:20, if net delta is more than 0.10 per strangle lot from flat, trade NIFTY futures to the nearest whole lot of flat.
      5. Exit. Hold to expiry. No stops, no profit targets.
      6. Size. At least 5 strangle lots, so whole futures lots can hedge finely. About ₹26 lakh at a 10% volatility target.

      • In 2023–26, strangle credits thinned to 0.7% of spot and the edge was about zero.

      • Every option-buying rule, including the dip-call (−3.8% a trade in 2008–13).

      • Far out-of-the-money unhedged selling: the 5-delta monthly call looked superb until the Oct–Nov 2026 rally.
      • Profit targets, 21-day exits and stops on the hedged book; Bank Nifty versions.
